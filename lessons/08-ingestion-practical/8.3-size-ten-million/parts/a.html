Your lane holds the kit's %%N_DOCS%% text documents. This lesson asks what the same kit would need for %%N%%: how many vectors, how many gibibytes of index, how many embedding requests, how many rupees and how many days. Every answer is arithmetic on three kinds of number: the kit's constants, the kit's corpus as the kit's own chunker counts it, and the prices and limits Google publishes. None of them is a guess, and every number on this page is computed from those three when the page is built.

The arithmetic is the easy half. The useful half is the four places where the kit, exactly as it is today, stops working long before ten million: two that grow faster than the corpus, and two that stay fixed while the corpus grows past them. You will read each one in the kit's code, and see what would change it.

Level 0 · Theory

What sizing a RAG system means

One average document turned into numbers, multiplied, and set beside every price and limit it meets.

Sizing a RAG system means turning one average document into the numbers each part of the system sees, then multiplying by the number of documents. In DocuMind a document becomes chunks, pieces of up to %%CHUNK_CHARS%% characters. Each chunk travels to the embedding model inside a request, comes back as a vector of %%DIMS%% numbers, goes into the Vector Search index, and is written to Firestore as a row. Count those for an average document, multiply by %%N%%, and put each total beside the price or the limit that applies to it. That is the whole method.

Three kinds of number go into it, and this page keeps them apart. The kit's constants set the rules: %%CHUNK_CHARS%%-character chunks with %%OVERLAP%% characters of overlap, at most %%EMBED_BATCH%% texts and about %%EMBED_TOKENS%% tokens in one embedding request, %%DIMS%% numbers a vector, %%IN_FLIGHT%% documents worked on at once, a nightly walk with %%WALK_S%% seconds to finish, one replica. The corpus sets the averages: the kit's %%N_DOCS%% text documents, chunked by the kit's own code, make %%N_CHUNKS%% chunks, %%PER_DOC%% a document. Google's prices and limits, which the kit does not hold, turn totals into rupees and machines: how much one shard of an index holds, what a serving node costs an hour, what 1,000 characters cost to embed. Those were read on %%PRICE_DATE%%, and step 8 links each one, because they change.

Most of what grows, grows in a straight line. Ten times the documents is ten times the vectors, the gibibytes and the rupees, and a straight line is easy to read once you have one point on it. Sizing is really the search for what does not grow that way. Two things in the kit grow faster than the corpus, because every new document makes them re-read the documents before it. Two others stay the same size while the corpus grows past them: a fixed number of documents in flight, and a fixed number of replicas. Step 8 reads all four in the kit's code.

🍛

A wedding caterer in Jaipur, planning for ten thousand guests from a tasting for twenty-seven. The recipe card says what one plate holds: two rotis, a katori of dal, a spoon of pickle. The tasting shows what twenty-seven guests actually ate, second helpings included. The mandi's rate list, dated that morning, turns kilos into rupees. Multiply, and the flour, the dal and the bill all grow in a straight line. Then the caterer looks at the kitchen. It has thirty burners, however many guests come. The head cook insists on recounting every plate already served before sending out the next one. One night-watchman must walk past every table before dawn. And there is one serving counter.

The recipe card is the kit's constants, the tasting is the kit's corpus counted by its own chunker, and the dated rate list is Google's price page. The thirty burners are the worker's %%IN_FLIGHT%% documents in flight. The head cook's recount is the corpus fingerprint, which reads every indexed document of a tenant on every ingest. The night-watchman is the nightly walk and its %%WALK_S%% seconds, and the serving counter is the index's one replica. At twenty-seven guests none of these matters. At ten thousand, each is the first thing to fail.

Ten million documents, from the kit's own numbers

Not on the calculator: what a question costs to answer, Document AI's and DLP's own prices, the storage Firestore's point-in-time recovery adds, and the sparse half and the filters every vector carries, which Google says take shard space too. So every figure is a floor. The four kinds are averages of the kit's own documents: %%SH_STAT_DOCS%% Acts and Codes, %%SH_HAND_DOCS%% handbooks and policies, %%SH_SHORT_DOCS%% short contracts, invoices, reports and transcripts, or all %%N_DOCS%% as the corpus mixes them.

Read the default first: %%N%% documents shaped like the kit's corpus, all in one tenant, on MEDIUM shards. The straight lines are big but ordinary. %%T_CHUNKS%% vectors make %%T_GIB%% GiB of index, which takes %%T_SH_M%% MEDIUM shards and costs %%T_SERVE_M%% a month to serve; embedding the corpus once costs %%T_EMBED%%. Two rows are orange, and they are the lesson. The fingerprint row says the load would read Firestore %%T_FP_READS%% times, which is %%T_FP%%, more than everything else on the page together. The ingest row says that at %%IN_FLIGHT%% documents at a time, Document AI alone would take %%T_DOCAI_DAYS%% days. Now move the tenants to 1,000: the fingerprint's reads fall about a thousandfold, because they grow with the square of each tenant's size, not with the corpus. Then move the documents down until both orange rows turn blue: that is roughly the size the kit, as it is, was built for.

Two pairs in this table are easy to confuse, and the lesson keeps them apart. A chunk is text and a vector is numbers: the bill for embedding counts characters, the bill for the index counts vectors. And a shard is a slice of the data while a replica is a copy of all of it: more data means more shards, more questions a second means more replicas, and the kit fixes the second at one.
