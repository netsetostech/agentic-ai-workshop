Direct reads: Firestore's counts and the worker's seconds

The rows the Firestore floor multiplies, and the one number no constant gives you: how long a document takes.

Two reads finish the measurement. The first counts rows in Firestore without fetching them: a count() query returns one number instead of the rows it matched.

%%CELL_COUNTS%% %%OUT_COUNTS%%

chunk rows counts every version a tenant still holds: the current rows, and the rows a re-issue retired, which stay until their expire_at passes. current is what the index serves; added across the three tenants, it should match the index's vector count from steps 3 and 6. indexed sources is how many rows the fingerprint reads on every ingest to that tenant, which step 8 comes back to. Multiply your chunk rows by %%CHUNK_B%% bytes and you have your lane's Firestore floor for the chunks; for the %%N_CHUNKS%% text chunks alone it is %%L_FS_MIB%% MiB. At ten million documents it is %%T_FS_TIB%% TiB.

The second read is the calibration. Cloud Run logs every delivery Pub/Sub made to the worker, with its HTTP status and how long it took, so the median of those times is your lane's seconds per document. The cell saves the last month of deliveries, then counts them by status:

%%CELL_LATENCY%% %%OUT_LATENCY%%

Each line is one HTTP status. A 2xx is a delivery the worker took and finished. A 429, if you see one, is Cloud Run turning a delivery away because no instance was free; Pub/Sub tries again later, and step 8 reads what that does at scale. Anything in the 500s is a delivery that failed and will be retried. The median of the taken deliveries mixes long PDFs with short text files, and with re-deliveries that found the document already current, so treat it as this corpus's rough figure, not a constant. The last line multiplies it by %%N%% and divides by the %%IN_FLIGHT%% documents the worker takes at once. If the cell prints nothing, your lane's ingest is older than the log keeps; the calculator's ingest row still holds: %%T_INGEST_DAYS%% days for every second a document takes.

Three things stand out. Embedding is a one-off, and a batch job would pay %%T_EMBED_B%% for it instead of %%T_EMBED%%. And the fingerprint line is larger than all the others put together. That one is not a pricing problem. It is the first of the four limits.

1. The fingerprint reads everything, on every ingest

After every successful ingest, the worker records the document in the ledger, then recomputes the tenant's fingerprint:

@@ingest_ok@@ @@fingerprint@@

corpus_fingerprint() streams every indexed source row of the tenant to hash their keys. The ledger row is written first, so the ingest of a tenant's k-th document reads k rows, and loading N documents into one tenant reads 1 + 2 + ... + N rows, which is N(N + 1)/2. That is the curve that grows faster than the corpus: double the documents and the reads go up four times. At %%N%% documents in one tenant it is %%T_FP_READS%% reads, %%T_FP%% at Firestore's Mumbai price of $%%FS_READ%% per 100,000 reads. Time breaks it as well: the last ingest has to stream %%N%% rows inside its %%TIMEOUT%%-second request, %%T_FP_RATE%% rows a second.

It overtakes the cost of embedding early. For the corpus's mix, embedding one document costs about as much as %%CROSS%% Firestore reads, so once a tenant holds more than %%CROSS%% documents, each new one costs more to fingerprint than to embed. For the statutes the crossing is at %%CROSS_STAT%% documents; for short documents, which are cheap to embed, it is at %%CROSS_SHORT%%. Spreading the same documents over more tenants flattens the curve, because each tenant's sum starts again from one, and the calculator's tenants control shows by how much.

2. The nightly walk must finish in %%WALK_S%% seconds

The reconcile job is the backstop for every event the worker missed. It is off unless you turn it on, and when it runs, it runs as one task, with no retry and a %%WALK_S%%-second limit:

@@walk_switch@@ @@walk_tf@@

Before it decides anything, it loads three complete lists: every object in the uploads bucket, every sources row and every documents row:

@@walk_py@@

At %%N%% documents that is %%T_WALK_ITEMS%% objects and rows, listed and held in one task's memory within %%WALK_S%% seconds: %%T_WALK_RATE%% a second, before the first comparison. The reads are cheap, %%T_WALK_RS%% a night. The limit is the single task and the fixed time. When the time runs out, the task is stopped, the job does not retry, no drift number is printed, and the kit's Nightly reconcile failed alert pages. This limit grows in a straight line, but against a ceiling that does not move.

3. %%IN_FLIGHT_CAP%% documents in flight

The worker takes one document per instance and runs at most %%IN_FLIGHT%% instances:

@@deploy_comment@@

So every second one document spends in the worker adds %%N%% ÷ %%IN_FLIGHT%% seconds to a ten-million-document load: %%T_INGEST_DAYS%% days. Your median from step 7 multiplies that. The kit itself states one floor for it, Document AI's pace on a PDF:

@@docai_pace@@

At roughly a second a page, and %%PAGES_DOC%% PDF pages in the corpus's average document, Document AI alone keeps a ten-million-document load running for %%T_DOCAI_DAYS%% days. Then there is what happens to the documents that wait. Pub/Sub delivers each upload's event at once; an event the worker cannot take is turned away and tried again, and the subscription counts every try:

@@dlq@@

The comment is the kit's own record that a refusal counts: the first live load sent a document to the dead-letter topic on five refusals, which is why the limit is now %%ATTEMPTS_W%%. %%ATTEMPTS_W_CAP%% deliveries span at most %%WAITS_W%% backoffs of at most %%MAX_BACKOFF%% seconds: %%DLQ_MIN%% minutes in all. Upload ten million documents at once and the last one waits %%T_BACKLOG_H%% hours for Document AI time alone. Any document refused %%ATTEMPTS_W%% times in those hours goes to the dead-letter topic, which lesson 8.1 reads, instead of the index. The limit was sized for thirty objects.

4. One replica

Step 4's excerpt fixes the deployed index at one replica, as both the minimum and the maximum. Replicas are what carry more questions a second. Google's sizing example on its pricing page, priced in Iowa, serves %%SIZING_V%% million vectors of %%SIZING_D%% dimensions at %%SIZING_Q1%% queries a second on one %%SIZING_M%% node, and at %%SIZING_Q3%% on three. The kit's index cannot add a second node when traffic grows. One replica also means no spare: a node that restarts leaves its shard unserved until it is back, and at %%T_SH_M%% MEDIUM shards there are %%T_SH_M%% such nodes.
