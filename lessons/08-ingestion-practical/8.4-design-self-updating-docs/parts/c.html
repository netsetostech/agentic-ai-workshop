So the monitoring catches two of the four: the poison twice, about half an hour in and again at the hour, and the backlog itself. A fifth, which no kit script reproduces: a claim cut off. release() gives a claim back when the pipeline raises. A worker that dies without raising, cut off at the request's %%TIMEOUT%% s or out of memory, never runs it, and the claim stays processing. Until 13 October 2026 claim() refused every later delivery and push() acked each one as a duplicate: the document was missing while every delivery succeeded, which is the warning release()'s own docstring gives, and the walk's nightly reingest was acked as a duplicate too. The design's answer was an age on the claim, and it is the kit's now: a claim holds for a lease counted from its claimed_at, %%LEASE%% s on the push lane (the ack deadline and a minute) and %%BATCH_LEASE%% s for a claim the batch job took. A delivery inside the lease is nacked with a 409 and comes back; the first one after it takes the claim back in a transaction and indexes the bytes, logged as ingest_claim_retaken, which the lifecycle metric counts. With the walk on, the night's rewrite is that delivery. The cut-off itself pages nothing; %%D_BACKLOG%% pages only if the retries outlast %%BACKLOG_MIN%% minutes.

Dimension 3 · Cost at 10×

Cost at 10×: the fingerprint breaks first

Ten times the bill is arithmetic. What stops being linear on the update path is a read that grows with the tenant, a walk with a fixed clock, and a burst the push lane cannot hold.

Definition

The commonest 2 on this dimension scales the bill by ten and stops. For a self-updating platform, ten times the load is ten times the changes, or a tenant ten times larger, and three limits on the update path stop being linear well before the index does. Lesson 8.3 found them in a ten-million-document load; here they are as limits on every change:

%%LIMITS_TABLE%%

So what breaks first at 10×? Take the middle scale and multiply it by ten: a tenant of a million documents. Every change, however small, re-reads the tenant's million ledger rows, %%FP_1M_RATE%% a second inside one %%TIMEOUT%%-second request, and Rs %%FP_1M_RS%% in reads; the walk must list %%WALK_1M_RATE%% objects and rows a second for %%WALK_S%% seconds. The fingerprint breaks first, because it is the one cost that grows with the tenant on every change. Past %%FP_CROSS%% documents in a tenant, it already costs more than the rest of a handbook-sized edit together. At the top scale it costs Rs %%FP_10M_RS%% a change and must stream %%FP_10M_RATE%% rows a second. Spreading documents over tenants divides it, which lesson 8.3's calculator shows; an incremental fingerprint ends it, folding each change's doc_key into a running value on the ledger row in the same write that records it.

A 3 sounds like this

"At ten times our largest tenant, the first limit is the corpus fingerprint: every change reads the tenant's whole ledger inside a %%TIMEOUT%%-second request, a million rows at a million documents. Before a tenant passes %%FP_CROSS%% documents we move to an incremental fingerprint. The second is compaction: at ten million documents, staying fresh costs as much as serving the index."

Dimension 4 · Evals

Evals: a change is a release, and the check that cannot fail

The gate runs before a document is uploaded, the version row moves with it, and the anchors check never sees a chunk.

Definition

The kit treats a document change the way it treats a code change. make reindex runs the offline gate before it uploads anything, then make eval-live SOURCE= judges the rows that cite the document on a candidate; deploy/INDEXING.md calls this "a reindex is a release". The gate is evals/run_eval.py: %%N_THRESH%% thresholds and %%N_REQUIRED%% required rows over %%N_GOLDEN%% golden rows. Its version row, vr-01, is this platform's own test. It asks lk-06's question and forbids the figure only revision 2 holds, so a re-issue that leaves the rows behind turns the gate red, and the rows move with the document in the same commit.

Then the rubric's hardest point: which checks cannot fail. For this platform, the offline anchors check is one. It looks for each must_retrieve anchor in the tenant's files, filename and whole text joined, and never in a chunk. No chunker change, no carry-over bug and no lost event can turn it red. What replaced it here is the live gate scoped to the document, whose rows are answered from the real index, and the lifecycle smoke, make smoke-reindex, which re-issues a fixture and puts it back. One gap remains: must_retrieve recall over the real pool is printed as information, not judged, so a chunker change is judged by the answers it changes, not by the chunks it loses.
