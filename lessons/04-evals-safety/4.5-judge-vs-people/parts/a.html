Level 0 · Theory

Agreement with chance taken out: people first, then the judge

Why a mean cannot say whether the judge is right, and how two readers, then the judge, are held to the same rows.

A judge's mean says how it rated, not whether it rated right. Lesson 4.2's judge ends in an Experiments run of means: the share of answers judged grounded, the average instruction-following score. Those numbers are only as good as the judge, and a judge is a prompt template with a model behind it. To know how far to trust one, you need people's labels on the same rows. Then the judge's verdict on each row can be set beside theirs, row by row, and counted.

People first. Before a judge is held to people, the people are held to each other. Two readers label the same rows from the same guide. If they split often, the question is unclear, or the guide is, and agreeing with either of them says little about a judge. So the kit measures two readers on a rubric before it measures the judge on it.

Raw agreement flatters; kappa takes chance out. Two readers who both say yes to nine rows in ten agree on most rows by chance alone. Cohen's kappa subtracts what chance would give: kappa = (p_o - p_e) / (1 - p_e), where p_o is the share of rows the two label alike and p_e the share they would label alike if each kept their own counts of yes and no and gave them out at random. Take the kit's worked table: %%W_N%% rows, %%W_TP%% both yes, %%W_FF%% and %%W_FP%% split, %%W_TF%% both no. The two readers label %%W_ALIKE%% of the %%W_N%% rows alike, so p_o is %%W_PO%%. But one says yes %%W_A_YES%% times and the other %%W_B_YES%%, so chance alone would give p_e = %%W_PE%%, and kappa is %%W_K%%. 1 is perfect agreement, 0 is no better than chance, and below 0 is worse than chance.

Then the judge, with people as the reference. Every row lands in one cell of a 2x2: true pass (people pass it, the judge passes it), false fail, false pass and true fail. Two rates come out of it. The true-pass rate is the share of the rows people pass that the judge passes too. The false-pass rate is the share of the rows people fail that the judge passes anyway: the bad answers it would let through, which is the error that matters when a judge vouches for answers. Groundedness is the judge's own 0 or 1. Its second metric, %%SDK_SECOND%% in the SDK that lesson 4.2 pins, is scored 1 to 5, so a cut turns it into pass or fail: CUT or more passes. The kit starts at %%CUT%%, and the cut is yours to move.

At twenty rows, every rate is a range. One row of twenty moves a rate by five points, so the kit prints each rate as k of n with a 95% Wilson interval. Suppose people fail %%THIRD%% of twenty rows, about a third, and the judge passes none of them: its false-pass rate is %%THIRD_0%%, and the true rate could still be as high as %%THIRD_0_HI%%. That is why the judge stays an adviser in this lesson, as it was in lesson 4.2.

📝

Moderating a board exam's marking. Before a new examiner marks board papers alone, the head examiner hands over twenty scripts that two senior examiners have already marked, without showing their marks. First the head examiner sets the two seniors side by side: if they split on many scripts, the marking scheme is unclear, and nobody can be judged against it yet. Then the new examiner's marks go beside the seniors'. Agreeing on scripts that everyone passes proves little. What counts is how many weak scripts the new examiner passed. Your sheet and the author's are the two seniors' marks, and the LLM judge is the new examiner.

The agreement board: two readers, or the judge against one

The board below holds the kit's judge fixture: %%FX_N%% synthetic rows, %%FX_BUILT%% of them constructed, two made-up readers, FX1 and FX2, and the ratings of the stand-in judge the kit's tests use. Pick a rubric and who to set against whom, tap a row to read it, and flip a label to see what one row does. Try these:

  • Two people, groundedness. FX1 and FX2 agree on %%FX_PG_AGREE%%. Chance alone would give %%FX_PG_CHANCE%%, so kappa is %%FX_PG_K%%. The one row they split on, fx-12, is constructed: FX2 passed an answer carrying a sentence its context never said.
  • Tap %%FX_FLIP%% and flip FX1's label. Both readers said yes to that row. Agreement falls to %%FX_FLIP_AGREE%% and kappa to %%FX_FLIP_K%%: one row of %%FX_N%% did that.
  • Two people, answers the question. Kappa is %%FX_PM_K%% again, and the split is fx-04: half an answer, which FX2 let through and the guide calls a no.
  • The judge against FX1, groundedness. Kappa %%FX_JG_K%%. The judge passes fx-07, an answer about a sabbatical its context never mentions: the stand-in looks only for digits, and this answer writes its numbers in words. It fails fx-06, a refusal that claims nothing, because the refusal names a year its context does not hold.
  • The judge, answers the question: drag the cut. At %%CUT%%, the false-pass rate is %%FX_JM4_FP%%. At 3, fx-04's half answer passes too: %%FX_JM3_FP%%, and kappa drops to %%FX_JM3_K%%. At 1 every row passes, and kappa is %%FX_JM1_K%%: a judge that passes everything agrees with people no better than chance.
%%BOARD_FX%%

The fixture's judge is not Gemini. The kit's tests rate these rows with a stand-in, a fixed rule, so that the tests run anywhere at Rs 0 and their numbers never move. It is crude on purpose, and its blind spot is the one a real judge can share: an unsupported sentence with no figure in it.

@@standin@@
What the board is not

It calls no model and reads no lane. The fixture's rows are synthetic, every question ends "(fixture)", and FX1 and FX2 were made up for the kit's tests, so its numbers are a worked example, not a verdict on any judge. From step 3 the same arithmetic runs on the frozen set: your labels, the author's, a peer's and the real judge's ratings, and step 5 puts the author's rows on this board.

One distinction to hold: agreement is not accuracy. Kappa says how far two readers agree beyond chance; it does not say which of them is right. People are the reference here because the rubric is theirs, not because a person cannot be wrong: on the board, FX2 passed a constructed sentence.
