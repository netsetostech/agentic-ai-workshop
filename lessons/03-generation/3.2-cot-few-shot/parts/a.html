Prompt with chain of thought and few-shot exemplars: room to think on the join rows, one reasoning rule against it, and the router's examples taken out and put back

Two ways to get a better answer from the same model. Chain of thought gives the model room to work before it answers. A Gemini 3 model does that natively: it writes thought tokens before its reply, which the kit never asks to see and pays for at the output rate, and the generator asks for little of it, thinking level %%KIT_LEVEL%%. A prompt can also ask for the working in writing, and under DocuMind's answer schema the working has one place to go: the answer the person reads. Few-shot puts solved examples in the prompt: the router's first classifier, %%L1_MODEL%% with a thinking budget of 0, reads %%N_EX_WORD%% questions, each labelled with its desk. Level 0 · Theory

Two ways to help a model answer: room to think, and solved examples

Chain of thought, native or prompted; few-shot against zero-shot; and why each is measured on the same rows with its interval.

Chain of thought is work written before the answer. A model writes one token at a time (lesson B.5), so whatever it writes before its answer is something the answer can build on. Golden row jn-03 asks "%%JN03_Q%%" The answer needs two clauses and one step between them: the handbook's cap on leave encashment, the notice period for an E3, and the question's %%JN03_DAYS%% days held against the cap. That is the bet chain of thought makes: a model that works those out first may get them right more often than one that starts writing the answer at once. Step 6 measures whether it does here. A Gemini 3 model does this natively: before its reply it writes thought tokens, billed at the output rate whether or not anyone reads them. thinking_level sets how much: MINIMAL, LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH. MEDIUM is %%GEN_MODEL%%'s default (lesson B.5 reads Google's model page), and the kit's generator pins %%KIT_LEVEL%%. Its request names the level and nothing else, so the thoughts never come back to the kit: only their count does.

Prompted reasoning writes the working where the person reads it. Before models thought natively, the way to get a chain of thought was to ask for it in the prompt, "work it out step by step", and let the model write its working into the reply. You can still do that, and it is the one change step 7 makes. In DocuMind it has a cost the textbook version does not. The generator asks for a ModelDraft, four fields, and google-genai sends them to the model in a fixed order: %%DRAFT_ORDER%%. None of them is for working. So working that the prompt asks for is written into answer, the text the Chat page shows. It is billed as visible output and counts toward rule 4's 300 words. Native thinking happens before the first field; prompted working happens inside it.

Few-shot shows the task instead of only describing it. A zero-shot prompt describes the task and its rules. A few-shot prompt also shows solved examples, each an input with the right output, and the model continues the pattern. The kit uses each where it fits. The generator's SYSTEM is zero-shot: five rules and no example, because every answer is different and so is every context. The router's first classifier, L1, is few-shot. It is %%L1_MODEL%% with a thinking budget of 0, an answer of five enum fields and no room to explain. It reads the desks, the rules and %%N_EX_WORD%% example questions, each labelled with its desk (desk_routes.PROMPT_EXEMPLARS, at most %%EX_MAX_WORD%%). Choosing one label out of five suits examples: a small model that does not think can copy the boundary the examples draw, between a handbook question and a statute one, or between the company's own question and another system's. Lesson 10.4 builds the router; this lesson takes its examples out and puts them back.

Measure each on the same rows, with an interval. A technique that helps on the question you tried can hurt on the next one, so the kit measures every change over a set of rows and prints every rate with its Wilson interval. On the %%N_JOIN%% join rows, %%N_JOIN%% of %%N_JOIN%% passing has the interval %%W11%%. Two levels' intervals stop overlapping only when their pass counts are at least %%W_GAP_WORD%% rows apart, and at that gap only at the two ends of the scale: %%W_SEP_PAIRS%%. So one run per level settles little about accuracy, while the thought tokens and the rupees per answer show up at once. The router's L1 sees %%N_L1%% turns a run, so its intervals are narrower. Both arms answer the same turns, so the turns that one arm gets right and the other gets wrong (step 10) say more than the two rates side by side. Lesson 3.1 measures how far one row's answer moves from run to run.

The new clerk at the HR helpdesk. A new clerk gets two kinds of help. For the hard question, "I am leaving with fifty days of leave: what do I get, and how long do I stay?", the supervisor says take a minute. The clerk works it out on a scratch pad, the pad goes in the bin, and the employee gets only the answer slip. The office pays for the clerk's minute whether or not anyone reads the pad, and a longer minute costs more: that is thinking, and its level. Or the supervisor says write your working on the slip. Now the employee reads the working, the slip is longer and costs more to write: that is prompted reasoning. At the sorting tray, where each letter goes to a counter, the supervisor does something else. Eight letters from last month are pinned above the tray, each stamped with the counter it went to, and the clerk sorts the next letter by its likeness to them: that is few-shot. To know whether any of it helps, the office manager does not ask the clerk. She runs the same files both ways and counts, and she knows that eleven files cannot tell a careful clerk from a lucky one.

A join, taken apart: what the answer needs from the documents

A join row is a golden question whose answer needs two facts. Thinking does not find a fact the pool lacks; it helps the model put found facts together. Pick one of the %%N_JOIN%% rows. The router's prompt, with and without its examples

The other technique, in the kit's own prompt. desk_router.prompt() builds the L1 classifier's prompt: the task, the %%N_DESKS_WORD%% desks each described by the documents it owns, %%N_L1_RULES_WORD%% rules, the examples, then the question, fenced as data. Turn the examples off: the swap that route_eval.py --exemplars none makes takes out exactly the %%N_EX_WORD%% example lines, and nothing else moves. Each question in the list is a scored dev row that reaches L1 in the kit's offline run; none is in an example's group.
