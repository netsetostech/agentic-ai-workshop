Module 3 · Generation · Lesson 3.1

See run-to-run variance and thinking levels in the same prompt: five runs of one golden row at LOW and HIGH, judged by the gate's rule

Ask DocuMind the same question twice and the answer can come back in other words. Lesson B.5 showed where that comes from, and why the kit sends its model no temperature. This lesson measures it on the kit itself. The prompt bench, evals/prompt_bench.py, reads one golden row's passages from your lane once, asks the kit's own generate() the row's question five times at thinking level LOW and five at HIGH, and judges every answer by the eval gate's own rule. It counts the distinct answers, the distinct sets of citations and the passes, keeps the thought tokens apart from the visible ones, and prices every answer in rupees. Then it sends a temperature of 2.0, keeps proof that the value went out, and lets you read what changed. You will see the variance in the UI, read the code that holds everything else fixed, run the bench for Rs 0 and then on your lane, and read what your lane logged.

golden row %%ROW%%
5 runs at LOW, 5 at HIGH
thinking_level %%KIT_LEVEL%%: the kit's
temperature 2.0, sent and read back
pass k/5, with its interval
make bench
Rs %%INR_PER_K_OUT%% per 1,000 thought tokens

Level 0 · Theory

Why one prompt answers in different words, and what thinking buys

What can differ between two runs of one prompt, which difference the gate fails, what the bench holds fixed so that the model is all that is left, and what a thinking level and a temperature change.

Lesson B.5 explained the mechanism; this page measures it on the kit. A decoder writes one token at a time, each drawn from a probability distribution, so two runs of one prompt can draw differently and write different answers. B.5 built that loop in numpy, showed the knobs that reshape the distribution (temperature, top-k and top-p), quoted Google's model page saying that %%MODEL%% ignores all three, and sent a bare prompt five times. This page does not repeat any of it. It asks the kit's own generator, on your lane's own passages, and judges every answer the way the eval gate judges /v1/query.

Three things can differ between two runs, and only one of them is a failure. The wording: "45 days are encashed" and "encashment is capped at 45 days" are one answer to a reader. The citations: which of the passages the answer cites. The verdict: whether the answer carries what the golden row requires. Row %%ROW%% asks: %%Q%% The gate requires 45, the cap on encashed leave in clause LV-07, and 60, the E3 notice period in clause NP-03, each as a figure on its own boundary, and citations that name acme's own, current documents. An answer that has all 50 days encashed has varied in the way that matters. The bench counts all three, per thinking level: distinct answers, distinct cite sets, and pass k of N.

What the bench holds fixed, so that the model is all that is left. A difference between two answers could come from retrieval (a different pool), from the prompt (a different text), or from the model. The bench removes the first two. It reads the row's pool once from /v1/passages, which runs the same door, retrieval and reranker as /v1/query and stops before the model, and every run answers from that one pool. The prompt is generate()'s own, built from that pool, and the bench prints the sha256 of its head, the rules every prompt starts with, so that you can see it is the text %%PV%% names. What is left is the model: what it draws, and how long it thinks before it answers.

A thinking level is how long the model thinks before it answers, and you pay for it. A Gemini 3 model writes thought tokens before its answer. Nobody reads them, and they are billed at the output rate: Rs %%INR_PER_K_OUT%% per 1,000 on %%MODEL%%, %%RATIO%% times the price of a prompt token. thinking_level sets how much it thinks: %%LEVELS%%. The kit sends %%KIT_LEVEL%% on every answer. %%ROW%% joins two clauses and needs one small calculation (50 days earned, at most 45 encashed), which is the kind of question where more thinking could change the verdict. The bench asks it five times at %%KIT_LEVEL%% and five at HIGH, and prints per level the passes, the thought tokens an answer and the rupees an answer, so that whether HIGH is worth it is read off one table. Five runs are a small sample: five passes out of five still has a Wilson lower bound of %%W55_LO%%, so every rate on this page is read with its interval.

A temperature the model ignores, sent on purpose. generator.py sends no temperature: its comment says %%MODEL%% ignores one, and lesson B.5 quotes the model page that says so. The bench can send one anyway. Its edit arm sends 2.0, the hottest value B.5's toy tried, beside the kit's own arm, and its recorder keeps the config each call went out with. So the run answers two questions apart: was 2.0 sent (the recorder's log), and did anything change (the table).

🍳

One recipe card, five cooks, one inspector at the pass. A restaurant kitchen hands five cooks the same recipe card and the same tray of ingredients, and asks each of them for the same thali. The five plates come back different: one cook puts the dal on the left, another adds coriander, a third folds the roti in half. None of that is a mistake. The inspector at the pass does not compare the plates with one another. She checks each plate against the order: the two items it names, in the quantities it names, made from this kitchen's tray. A plate with three rotis where the order said two goes back, however well it is laid out. Give the cooks ten minutes to plan before they start and the plates may come out more alike, or more often right; the planning is paid by the minute either way, and nobody eats it. And the stove has a knob marked "flame" that, on this model of stove, is not connected to anything. Turn it to full and the cooking does not change, though the order slip still records that you turned it.

The recipe card is the prompt: generate()'s rules, the packed passages and the question. The tray is the row's pool, read once from /v1/passages. The five cooks are five calls at one thinking level. The plating is the wording, and the items named are the citations. The inspector is run_eval.row_verdict(), and the order's two quantities are 45 and 60. The planning time is the thinking level, paid in thought tokens. The knob is the temperature on %%MODEL%%, and the order slip is the bench's recorder.

The gate's rule, answer by answer

Before the bench runs, see what its judge does with wording. The widget below is run_eval's own reading of a figure, normalise() and contains(), and the text half of row_verdict(), ported to the browser and checked against the kit on %%N_CHECKED%% cases when this page was built. Try an answer in the first box: a chip fills it with one, and the lines under it say what the gate makes of it. Then edit the answers below it and watch three things move: each verdict, the count of distinct answers, and pass k/5 with its interval.

The gate's rule on golden row %%ROW%%: 45 and 60, each on its own boundary

A verdict here is the gate's for a reply whose citations are valid. A figure must stand on its own boundary once number words have become digits on both sides, so "sixty" is 60, "160" is not, and neither is "60.5". Distinct answers counts the texts that still differ once their spaces are collapsed, as the bench counts them, and pass k/5 carries its Wilson interval, as the bench prints it.

What the widget is not

It is the text half of the rule, not all of it. On your lane row_verdict() also checks every citation: that its chunk id and its object belong to acme, and that its version is one the ledger lists as current (judge_citations(), step 4). The widget takes the citations as valid, so an answer it passes could still fail on the lane for a citation; a recorded answer that did keeps the bench's own verdict until you edit it. It calls no model. And the "45 rupees, 60 hours" chip is there to show the rule's blind spot: it checks figures, not meaning. That is why the gate checks citations as well, and why lesson 4.2 adds a judge.
