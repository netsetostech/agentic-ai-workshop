Interview Q&A: the ledger

Ten questions an interviewer asks about idempotent ingestion, version swaps and undo - the ground idempotency.py stands on. Each answer gives the mechanism, the failure it prevents, the number, and what to say when pushed.

Interviewers who ask about document pipelines are rarely testing whether you know Firestore. They are testing whether you have been paged for an index that quietly went wrong: a document indexed twice, an old version resurrected by a late event, an undo that deleted what it meant to restore, a job that re-ingested what a person had just taken down. Good answers name a mechanism, the failure it prevents, and the number that proves it - reused=281 embedded=2 retired=283, 30 days, 400 writes, 250 pages - and stop.

Conceptual · Q1

Why is the idempotency key derived from the document's content, and why does taking the claim need a transaction?

The key is tenant_sha256, the hash of the bytes scoped to the tenant, because the two duplicates that matter arrive differently. A Pub/Sub redelivery carries the same message id, so a message-id key would catch it; the same PDF uploaded twice by two people is two messages with two ids, and that is the duplicate users actually create. A content hash catches both. The tenant prefix matters as much: until 7 September 2026 the chunk ids were not tenant-scoped, and two tenants uploading the same Act overwrote each other's rows - ACME lost seven shared Acts to whichever tenant ingested last.

The claim, documents/{doc_key}, is taken in a Firestore transaction because read-then-write is a race with a window of milliseconds, and push delivery hands duplicates to two workers concurrently: both read "absent", both write, the document is embedded twice. The transaction makes the read and the write one unit; the second writer's set fails and claim() returns False. If pushed: a crash between claim and finish must write failed, and failed must be retakeable, or every retry is acked as a duplicate and the document is missing with nothing alerting - the first live load.

Conceptual · Q2

Your ledger has three "gone" states: superseded, retired and withdrawn. Why three, and why does a person's decision need its own?

They answer three questions. superseded is on the per-version claim: a re-issue retired this version, and the undo window runs from its retired_at. retired is on the ledger row: the object left the bucket - storage absence, repaired by the nightly walk the moment the object is back. withdrawn is on the same row: a person ran make retire. The object is kept, the rows are flagged and expiring, and nothing automatic brings it back - the walk says "withdrawn, object kept", the same bytes are acked as ingest_withdrawn, reactivate() refuses, and only make restore ends it.

The third state exists because of a real night. The first walk had two states and recorded a hand retirement as retired; the next night it read "retired in the ledger but back in the bucket" - the object had never left - and re-ingested what a person had taken down twelve hours earlier. An absence and a decision look identical in storage; they must not look identical in the ledger. If pushed: make restore moves the row to retired first, the state a lost event recovers from, and then rewrites the object, so a lost finalize event still gets repaired.

Conceptual · Q3

"Retire, never delete; purge by policy." Who deletes a chunk row, and why not the worker?

Nothing on the code path deletes. A retired row is flagged - current: false, superseded_by, superseded_at - and stamped expire_at = now + RETENTION_DAYS, 30 by default. The only deleter is a Firestore TTL policy on chunks.expire_at, declared in Terraform (google_firestore_field.chunks_expire_at, ttl_config {}) and run by the platform about a day after the stamp. A staged row that nothing swapped carries a one-day stamp and leaves the same way.

Three things follow. The audit story survives: a retired row still says which version replaced it and when. The undo is free: the same bytes again flip the flag back and embed nothing, because the rows are still there. And the retention window and the undo window are one number, read from one Terraform variable by the worker and the reconcile, so they cannot disagree. Why not let the worker delete? A delete is the one write you cannot take back, and the worker runs at-least-once on a queue that redelivers; a flag written twice is still a flag. If pushed: make purge is the manual twin for a lane without the policy - it prints by default, deletes only with APPLY=1, and on a lane with the policy finds nothing.

Practical · Q4

The HR handbook, 283 chunks, is re-issued with one clause changed. What is embedded, and how do you know from the log?

The new bytes hash to a new doc_key, so the claim is won. The chunker cuts by ## section, so 281 of the 283 chunks have the same text as before, and each carries chunk_hash, the sha256 of its whitespace-collapsed text. The carry-over reads the previous version's current rows, matches by hash, and reuses a vector only when the row's stamp matches the worker's - model, version and task type RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT. The two changed chunks are embedded. The new rows are written staged, one pass swaps them current and retires the old 283 with a 30-day expire_at, and the ledger row is rewritten.

The log says it on one line: ingest_ok with chunks 283, reused 281, embedded 2, retired 283, preceded by ingest_superseded naming the retired key and expire_days 30; make sources shows the same three counts on the row. Cost: two embedding calls of a few hundred tokens - a fraction of a rupee against 283 for a naive re-index, and no Document AI because the handbook is Markdown. If pushed: the number that would worry me is reused 0 - the chunker moved every boundary (fixed windows over the whole file did exactly that) or the embedding stamp changed under us.

Practical · Q5

The same old bytes arrive again 40 days after they were superseded, and the retention is 30 days. What happens, exactly?

The claim for the old key is refused - its status is superseded - so the worker treats the delivery as a possible undo. It checks the tombstone (not withdrawn), then calls reactivate(), which counts before it flips: the retired rows still present against the claim's chunks, and the claim's retired_at against RETENTION_DAYS. The stamp is 40 days old and the TTL policy has almost certainly taken the rows, so it logs reactivate_incomplete with rows, chunks, age_days and retention_days, and returns None having changed nothing.

The worker does not stop. It takes the claim back with claim(retake=True), which admits a superseded record, and runs the ordinary pipeline: the bytes are chunked, the carry-over reuses every vector the newer version still holds by chunk_hash, the rest are embedded, and the swap retires the newer version only once the new rows are whole. The line is ingest_ok, not ingest_reactivated. If pushed on the first version: it flipped whatever remained and retired the newer version regardless, and after the window that left a source with no current version at all.

Practical · Q6

A worker fails between taking the claim and finishing. What do you see, and how does the system recover without a person?

Inside index_document() every exception is caught once: release() sets the claim to failed with the first 400 characters of the error, an ingest_failed line carries the same text and the lane, and the handler answers 500. Pub/Sub treats the 500 as a negative acknowledgement and redelivers with backoff, up to twelve attempts, then parks the message in the dead-letter topic. The next delivery's claim() wins because failed is in the retake set, and the document is processed from the start; nothing was visible, because the staged rows were never swapped, and a stage nobody swaps expires in a day.

What alerts: alerts.tf turns the ingest events into a logs-based metric, and the Ingest failed policy pages on the line within ten minutes, with the commands in the notification. A true crash - the process killed before the except runs - leaves the claim at processing, the one state no retry can take; it is visible in documents/, fixed by setting it failed by hand and re-uploading. If pushed: that is exactly why the failure-to-release bug was found on the first live load - every retry acked as a duplicate.

Practical · Q7

A 400-page contract is uploaded. Trace it through the batch lane and tell me what can go wrong.

The push handler wins the claim, then reads the page count off the PDF's page tree with pypdf before paying for any OCR. Over MAX_INLINE_PAGES - 250, because Document AI runs at about a second a page inside a 600-second request - it writes ingest_batch/{doc_key} with the object, its generation, its type and the page count, sets the claim to queued, and acks with ingest_queued_batch naming the consumer. If BATCH_JOB names the job, the worker starts documind-ingest-batch through the Cloud Run Jobs API as its own identity; an hourly schedule at :15 IST drains the queue regardless. The job takes the claim with take_batch() in a transaction, downloads the bytes by the recorded generation, and runs the same index_document() with lane="batch" and a two-hour task timeout.

What can go wrong: a redelivery while it waits is acked as queued_batch, not a duplicate; the walk reports the generation as queued and does not rewrite it; an object overwritten before the job reads it is recorded as gone; bytes whose hash no longer matches the claim are refused. A failure leaves the claim failed and the job never retakes it - make reindex or the same bytes again is the way back. If pushed: nothing in the kit's corpus crosses 250 pages (the CGST Act is 236), so the lane is proved by lowering MAX_INLINE_PAGES on a candidate, not by finding a bigger Act.

System design · Q8

Design the visibility of a new version so that a reader never sees two versions of one document, without a distributed lock.

Write the new version invisible, flip it in one pass, and make the reader tolerant of the one moment the pass cannot cover. The new rows land with current: false and staged: true, so no query that filters on current finds them. swap_versions() runs two loops in one batch: the new rows to current: true first, then every other current row of the source retired with superseded_by, superseded_at and expire_at. Up to 400 writes commit together; a longer document takes two commits, and between them both versions are current.

That window is closed on the read side, which is cheaper than a lock and needs no coordination: prefer_current() drops every row with current: false, and newest_per_source() keeps one doc_key per source_uri - the one whose rows landed last by indexed_at or reactivated_at. Because the new rows were flipped first, the worst a reader sees is the new version alone; never none, never both. The ANN tier follows with a current restrict on every datapoint. If pushed on a Firestore transaction instead: it caps at 500 writes and cannot include Vector Search, so it would promise atomicity for exactly the documents that cannot have it.

System design · Q9

Events can be lost. Design the reconciliation that makes a lost event a delay instead of a hole, and say what you alert on.

Keep a ledger keyed by object path - sources/{tenant~name} with the current doc_key, generation and sha - and walk the bucket against it on a schedule: a Cloud Run job on the ingest image at 23:30 IST. The planner is pure: given the bucket's current generations, the ledger and the claims, it returns one action per object and per row. An object the ledger never saw, or whose generation moved, is hashed: the ledger's own sha is a touch; a sha the claims already hold is a backfill; otherwise a reingest, done by rewriting the object onto itself so the same event and the same worker handle it - one ingestion path, not two. A ledger row whose object is gone is retired: flagged, stamped, never deleted.

The walk ends on one number, drift: retires plus reingests plus backfills. A withdrawn source and a queued document are decisions, not drift, and are counted beside it. The number becomes a logs-based metric, and the policy pages when it stays above zero for two nights - one night's drift is a lost event, two nights' is a lane nobody is reconciling; a second policy pages when the job's task fails. If pushed: the walk plans from the ledger, never from what it did last night, so it is idempotent against itself, and its selftest runs the planner with no project.

Trade-off · Q10

Firestore and Vector Search never share a transaction here. Defend that, and tell me what you gave up.

They cannot share one: Firestore's transaction is its own, and a Vector Search upsert is an API call with its own consistency. So the design orders the writes and puts the tolerance in the reader. Firestore is the truth - staged rows, the swap, the flags. The ANN tier follows: new datapoints after the swap, retired ids removed after. The undo repairs the tier first (reupsert from the rows' own vectors) and retires the newer version second, so the tier never holds none, and every datapoint carries a current restrict, so even before the removal a query for current rows does not return a retired id.

What was given up: a window of seconds in which the tier and the rows disagree, and a failure mode in which a crashed worker leaves the tier holding datapoints Firestore has retired. Both are bounded and repairable - the reader filters on the rows' flags, and make backfill-vectors rebuilds the tier from Firestore, which holds every embedding. The alternative, Vector Search as the truth, would put the retention window, the undo and the audit trail in a store with none of those primitives. If pushed on the repair: it uses optimistic concurrency on the rows, upserts before it checkpoints, and runs in a maintenance window, because a failed ANN write must never mark a row migrated.

How to prepare

Read idempotency.py once end to end and be able to name, for each function, the row it writes and the log line it leaves. Run make smoke-reindex on a lane and keep the three lines - ingest_ok, ingest_superseded, ingest_reactivated - with their numbers; interviewers trust a candidate who quotes their own log. Then practise the one sentence: the undo embedded nothing because the rows were flagged, not deleted, and the flag was flipped back only after the count and the clock agreed.
